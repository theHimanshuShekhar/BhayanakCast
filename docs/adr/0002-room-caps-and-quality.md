# 2. Room caps and adaptive stream quality

Date: 2026-09-27 · Status: accepted · Partly superseded 2026-10-11 (the fixed screen ladder, `captureCap`, the download estimate and the heaviest-first step-down; see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps") · Codec order amended 2026-10-22 (see "Addendum: codecs by what the streamer's machine can afford")

## Context
Under the P2P mesh (ADR 1), each streamer uploads one copy of their screen share per viewer, so the streamer's uplink is the limiting factor.

## Decision
- Rooms cap at **10 people**, with at most **3 simultaneous streamers** (screen shares).
- Screen-share quality ladder: **low 720p30 → default 1080p30 → max 1080p60**, with intermediate steps. *Superseded 2026-10-11: steps are now built for each share from its capture, up to about 3.7 MP (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps").*
- Quality is auto-negotiated and continuously adjusted per peer connection based on live connection stats (bandwidth estimate, packet loss, RTT), moving anywhere between low and max.
- Each peer pair negotiates the best codec both sides support (SDP codec preference ordering, e.g. AV1 > VP9 > H.264 > VP8, subject to hardware encode support).

## Consequences
- **Bandwidth assumption:** users have at least 25 Mbps symmetric, and on average 100 Mbps or more.
- Worst-case upload is a streamer sending to 9 peers: 1080p30 is roughly 2.5–4 Mbps per copy (~25–35 Mbps). Their camera and mic add ~5 Mbps. Average (100 Mbps) users comfortably sustain 1080p30, and often 1080p60. Users at the 25 Mbps floor adapt down towards 720p30 when streaming to a full room.
- Worst-case download is 3 streams at 1080p60 (~20 Mbps) plus 9 cameras (~5 Mbps), which fits within the floor. *Superseded 2026-10-11: a share can now be 10.67 Mbps (2560×1440 or an ultrawide at 60 fps), so 3 shares and 9 cameras come to about 37 Mbps, which does not fit the floor. A viewer's receive side is not limited by anything yet; that is for #99 to decide ("How should the quality controller react to a viewer whose incoming bandwidth is short?").*
- Because every link is a separate RTCPeerConnection, quality and codec can differ per viewer — a viewer on a bad link does not degrade others.
- Design copy mentioning "10–15 people" and 15-person capacities must change to 10.

## Addendum: cameras and mics
- Anyone in a room may turn on their camera. Camera default is 360p15, adaptively dropping to 180p under pressure.
- A camera track is paused towards any peer that is not currently rendering that tile (hidden viewers, collapsed layout).
- Mic audio uses Opus for every peer.

## Addendum: screen-share audio
Screen shares request audio through `getDisplayMedia`. Where the browser provides it (tab audio in Chromium; system audio on Windows), it is sent as a separate Opus track at ~128 kbps stereo with voice-processing (AEC/NS/AGC) disabled. Each viewer has their own volume control per share.

## Addendum: screen-share tuning (2026-09-29)
- A share starts only once the server accepts it (at most 3 streamers); the browser's screen picker opens after that, and a share the user cancels there ends at once.
- Browsers open the picker only within a few seconds of the user's click. The client waits at most 3 seconds for the server, and checks `navigator.userActivation` before opening the picker. If either is too late, the share ends and the user sees "couldn't start sharing — try again". A share the browser refuses otherwise (a cancelled picker) ends silently.
- **A cancelled share still counts.** The room saw it as LIVE from the server's acceptance, so it keeps its stream interval (a few seconds long) and its "started sharing" and "stopped sharing" feed entries. Not recording it would need another protocol step: the client would confirm capture before the hub opens the interval, and everyone's LIVE tile would wait for that. That isn't worth it for a few seconds.
- The share's video track gets a `contentHint` from the room kind: `motion` for gaming, watch-party and art rooms; `text` for code rooms; `detail` for music and chat rooms. Its sender's `degradationPreference` follows the hint: `maintain-framerate` for motion, `maintain-resolution` for detail and text.
- Capture asks for at most 1920×1080 at 60 fps, the ladder's top rung; each peer's sender encodes at the rung its connection allows (see the adaptive quality addendum). *Superseded 2026-10-11: capture is the screen's native size up to about 3.7 MP, at up to 60 fps.*
- Share audio is marked `music` and sent with `maxBitrate` 128 kbps. Browsers only encode stereo Opus at that rate when the receiving side asks for it, so the sender adds `stereo=1; maxaveragebitrate=128000` to the Opus parameters of its share-audio m-section in the remote description it applies. The mic stays at the browser's voice defaults.
- Everyone plays a share only while the server says the person is sharing, so a share a host, mod or admin stopped disappears for every viewer even if media still arrives (ADR 15).

## Addendum: adaptive quality and codecs (2026-09-29)
- **Ladders.** A screen share moves along 720p30 (1.5 Mbps), 900p30 (2.2), 1080p30 (3, the default), 1080p45 (4.5) and 1080p60 (6). A camera moves between 360p15 (the default, 500 kbps) and 180p15 (150 kbps). A rung is applied to one peer's sender with `setParameters` (`maxBitrate`, `maxFramerate`, `scaleResolutionDownBy` from the capture's height), so every viewer has a ladder of their own. A rung the capture can't feed (a 720p window; a 30 fps capture) is above the cap and never used. *Superseded 2026-10-11 for screens: the fixed ladder and the height-based cap (`captureCap`) are gone, and a share's steps are built from its capture (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps"). The camera ladder stands.*
- **Controller.** Every 4 s the Mesh reads each video sender's `getStats()` (bandwidth estimate, the bitrate actually sent, loss the receiver reports, round trip, the encoder's quality-limitation reason). `step` and `nextRung` (src/lib/quality.ts) are pure functions of the newest samples and the current rung.
  - *Pressure* is loss of 5% or more, a round trip of 500 ms or more, a CPU limit, or a bandwidth limit while the estimate is below 80% of what is being sent. Sending little (a still screen) is never pressure, and neither is a bandwidth limit that the rung's own `maxBitrate` puts on the encoder: Chromium's estimate only probes up to about the configured cap, so it says nothing about headroom above it.
  - It steps down after 2 pressured samples in a row, as far as the estimate says it must (net of what the peer's other video sender sends).
  - It steps up one rung as a probe after a window of 5 clean samples in a row (about 20 s; 2 in a share's first minute: see "Addendum: a seeded estimate and a faster first minute"), without asking the estimate for headroom. If the probe shows pressure, the step down brings it back and the next probe waits twice as long (up to 8 times the window); a probe that stays clean for a window resets the wait.
  - The window empties whenever the rung changes, and the first 2 readings of a new or resumed sender are ignored while the estimate ramps up (1 in a share's first minute: see "Addendum: a seeded estimate and a faster first minute"). A rung is applied per sender one change at a time and checked against the encoding on every pass, so a refused change or a resized capture heals by itself.
  - **The thresholds and windows are provisional**, to be tuned after the real-world session (#52); CI only sees localhost.
- **Codecs.** Each side tells the other which video codecs it can decode, in its `hello` and its descriptions (MIME types only). Each side then orders its own senders' codecs with `setCodecPreferences`: AV1, VP9, H.264, VP8 (others after them, then retransmission formats), leaving out what the peer can't decode. It takes effect in the next offer; before the peer has spoken, everything is offered in the same order and the answer keeps the best codec both have. The result is the same whichever side hears the other first. The mixed Chromium↔Firefox choice is covered by unit tests only: e2e pairs are always the same browser. Browsers that lack a codec (Firefox without AV1) simply don't list it. Audio stays Opus. Signalling sizes stay far below the 64 KB cap (ADR 4 addendum): about 24 KB for Chromium's largest offer with all eight m-sections.
- **Dev overlay.** In development builds only (`import.meta.env.DEV`), the room shows each peer's current rung and codec at the bottom left of the stage. Production builds don't contain it.
## Addendum: one uplink for all the pairs (2026-10-02)
- **The problem.** Every pair adapted on its own connection's stats, so a streamer with many viewers started every pair at 1080p30 (3 Mbps each, 27 Mbps for nine), saturated the uplink, and then every pair probed up again independently. The pairs also share one uplink, which no single pair's stats can show.
- **The budget.** The Mesh owns an `UplinkBudget` (src/lib/quality.ts): the most that the target bitrates (the rung's `maxBitrate`) of all the streamer's video senders, screens and cameras, may add up to. It starts at 23 Mbps: this ADR's 25 Mbps floor less 2 Mbps kept for audio (share audio and mics to nine viewers come to about 1.7 Mbps, and aren't in the budget). It then follows measurements:
  - *New pairs*, and a camera shown again after it was paused, start at the highest rung they would otherwise have that the other senders leave room for (never below the lowest rung, so a crowded room can start over budget). The next pass steps the heaviest senders down, a rung at a time, until the targets fit. *Superseded 2026-10-11: cameras step down first, then the screen furthest above its pair's ceiling (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps").* A pair whose stats read is still running (the per-peer guard, #74) is left out of that, and counts in the budget with what it was last measured sending.
  - *Strain on the uplink* is strain (loss of 5% or more, a round trip of 500 ms or more, or a bandwidth limit the estimate backs; a CPU limit is not strain, since no uplink cures it and it shows on every pair at once) on at least two pairs and at least half of them, in 2 passes in a row. One viewer's bad link stays that pair's own business: it steps down alone and the budget doesn't move. When the uplink is short, the budget drops to 90% of the bitrate the senders were measured sending then (the sum of each sender's `bytesSent` between readings), but by no more than 30% at a time, since a still screen sends next to nothing whatever the uplink does. The senders then step down to fit it.
  - *Probing up* goes ahead when the next rung fits the room left in the budget. *Superseded 2026-10-11 for screens: a probe within the pair's own ceiling goes ahead whatever the room, and pairs above theirs step down for it (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps").* Beyond it, a probe is admitted only after a clean spell (5 passes without strain on the uplink), and only for the sender with the lowest rung among those that can probe now (a connected pair, clean for a window, below its top step), so a viewer on a mediocre link, or a pair that isn't connected, never holds up the others. The budget grows by just that probe, so past it one pair at a time tries, and it recovers after strain clears. If strain returns before a growth has held for a window, the next clean spell is twice as long (up to 8 times), as for a pair's own probes.
- **`nextRung` and `step` stay pure.** A probe is only a proposal: the Mesh asks the budget before taking it, and if refused the sender's window carries on as it was.
- **Why the budget is not just the bandwidth estimate.** Each connection has its own estimate (`availableOutgoingBitrate`), and the estimates of pairs that share a link are not additive. They also only probe up to about the configured cap (see above), so they say nothing about headroom. The estimate still guides a pair's own step down; what the whole uplink carries is read from what was actually sent when strain is seen, and from clean running otherwise.
- **Provisional.** As for the rest of the controller, the 23 Mbps start, the 90% share, the 30% bound, the "two pairs and half of them" rule and the spells are guesses to be tuned after the real-world session (#52).
- **Encoder parameters are applied only when they change.** A browser that doesn't report `degradationPreference` in `getParameters()` (Firefox, probably Safari) never matched what a screen sender wanted, so `setParameters` ran for every sender on every pass (every 4 s). The comparison now takes such a browser to have what was last set on that sender.

## Addendum: quality telemetry (2026-10-11)
The thresholds above are guesses, and blur comes and goes (one viewer sees every share soft while the others are fine), so every browser in every room records what its video looks like on the wire. There is no notice in the app (the operator's choice); the data is visible to admins only (`/admin`, per room), stored for 90 days (ADR 11 addendum).
- **What.** Each browser reports one row per video sender (screen or camera, to each peer) and one per video receiver (from each peer), every 30 seconds, as averages and deltas over that window, and one more at every rung change, which ends the window with the rung it was on, the one it moved to, and why (`pressure`, `probe`, `capture`, `budget`, `resume`). A row has the rung and its `maxBitrate`, the encoder's `targetBitrate`, sent or received bitrate, `availableOutgoingBitrate`, average QP (Δ`qpSum`/Δframes, comparable only within one codec), the codec, frame size and fps on both ends, `qualityLimitationReason`, loss and round trip, PLI, FIR and NACK counts and keyframes, the receiver's freezes, the selected candidate pair (local and remote `candidateType`, `relayProtocol`), and the browser and its major version (from the user agent, on the server).
- **Both ends report**, because Firefox senders lack `qualityLimitationReason`, `targetBitrate` and `availableOutgoingBitrate`: what a browser lacks is stored as null, never 0, and the receiver's rows are then the only view of that pair. A sender's `qualityLimitationReason` stays `none` under `maintain-resolution` even when starved, which is why `targetBitrate` against the rung's `maxBitrate` is recorded (the admin table flags such a pair as starved).
- **Cadence.** It rides the existing 4-second quality pass (`Mesh.adjust`), which also runs for a page that sends nothing; senders are read there already, receivers add one `getStats` each per pass. A window closes at the first pass within 2 seconds of 30.
- **Transport.** Rows go to the server as batched server-function calls (`reportQualityFn`, like the connection events of ADR 3's addendum), not over the room socket: the hub runs every message on one serial queue and this is database work (ADR 4 addendum on flooding), and a report that fails costs a few rows instead of a reconnect. A page sends at most one report every 5 seconds, 64 rows each.
- **Trust.** The server takes a report only from a signed-in caller who is in that live room, keeps only rows about peers in it, dates rows by its own clock, takes the room's kind from the room, and refuses a user more than 1,500 rows in 10 minutes.
- **QP across codecs.** A window ends when a stream's codec changes, so a row's QP is of one codec, and the admin table averages QP over the pair's latest codec only.
- **The codec reason and the encoder** (#135) are added to a sender's rows by the codecs addendum below.
- **Stalls.** Only shares in progress are reported. A receiver of a screen or camera its peer is still sharing (the server's `media.share` / `media.cam`, the same state that decides whether the room plays it, per the screen-share tuning addendum above; a camera this page has hidden is paused towards it and doesn't count) that has received something and then gets nothing still reports (a bitrate of 0); one that never received anything reports no rows. When the peer stops sharing, the receiver sends what its window holds once, as a last (partial) row, and no more rows follow, so a share that ended isn't taken for a stalled one; a new share by the same peer starts a new stream, counting only the bytes that arrive after it was first read. A reconnect drops a pair's open windows but not what is known of who shares what. A rung-change row that ends a zero-length window has null counts and rates, not 0.
- **Leaving the page loses data.** A page that leaves the room (or closes) drops what it hadn't sent: its last partial windows, and the finished rows still waiting out the 5-second batching delay. The server would refuse them anyway, since the page is no longer in the room.

## Addendum: budget-driven quality steps (2026-10-11)
**Supersedes** the fixed screen ladder (720p30 → 1080p60) and `captureCap`, which capped the ladder by the capture's *height*. The first production telemetry (#97) showed the cost: a streamer on a 3440×1440 ultrawide was captured at 1920×804, `captureCap` took every rung above 720p to be above the capture, and the share sat on 720p30 at 1.5 Mbps for the whole session with about 4 Mbps of estimated bandwidth unused. The camera ladder (but for the camera's capture height, which still caps it), the codec order, the controller's thresholds and its step-down on pressure, the probe and its backoff, the first-readings ignore and the CPU-pressure handling are unchanged. The budget's accounting changes in three ways, below: fair shares, cameras first, and entitled probes.
- **Budget.** The streamer's budget is the measured `UplinkBudget` above (23 Mbps to start). The streamer sets nothing.
- **Ceiling per pair.** A pair's ceiling is the lower of its **fair share** of the budget and **what its own link carries** (`ceilingOf`, `pairCeilings` in src/lib/quality.ts). It is worked out in one place, so that a third term, the viewer's receive share, can be added later (#99). The fixed top of 1080p60 / 6 Mbps goes.
- **Capture.** The screen's native size up to about 3.7 MP (2560×1440, 3,686,400 pixels), at up to 60 fps. The cap is by pixel count, not width: 3440×1440 becomes 2968×1242, not 1920×804. `getDisplayMedia` constraints can't say "at most 3.7 MP"; width and height maxes only bound a box, and the browser scales a larger screen down into it keeping its shape. A box of 2970×1440 fits a 16:9 and a 21:9 screen at about the cap, but a 32:9 screen would get 2970×836 (67% of it) and a portrait 1440p one 810×1440 (32%). So where the browser allows it the first request is a box of up to 3840×3840, which delivers the screen at its own shape (a 5120×1440 screen comes as 3840×1080), and the track is then given `applyConstraints` with the box that fits that shape to the cap (`fitCapture`: 3620×1018 for that screen, 2560×1440 for 4K), or the fixed 2970×1440 box if that is refused (`fitScreenCapture` in src/lib/local-media.ts). A capture already under the cap is left alone. Chromium does this (checked in the e2e on a canvas track, whose `applyConstraints` scales like a screen's; Chromium's fake screen returns whatever box it is given, so it can't show the shape); **Firefox keeps the fixed 2970×1440 box**, because resizing a screen track there is unverified (headless Firefox has no screen to capture, and its canvas stand-in refuses `applyConstraints`), so a 32:9 or portrait screen in Firefox stays under the cap. Either way the steps fit the capture to the cap by pixel count themselves and scale down from what was captured with `scaleResolutionDownBy`, which is the same in both.
- **Steps.** Steps are built for each share from its capture (`screenSteps`), lowest first. Each is a size times a frame rate of 30 or 60 (60 only if the capture delivers it). The top size is the fitted capture. Below it come the capture's shape at the pixel counts of 1080p, 900p and 720p, as far as they are well below the top (under 80% of its pixels), so an ultrawide gets ultrawide-shaped steps and a small window gets fewer (a 1280×720 window has one size) and is never upscaled.
- **Bitrate.** `maxBitrate` is pixels × frames per second × **0.0482 bits per pixel** (3 Mbps ÷ 1920×1080×30, so that 1080p30 is 3 Mbps), rounded to 10 kbps. For 16:9 this is about today's numbers (720p30 1.33 Mbps, 900p30 2.08, 1080p30 3, 1080p60 6); 1440p60 is 10.67 Mbps; 2968×1242 at 60 fps is 10.67.
- **Floor.** The lowest step is about 720p's pixels (0.92 MP) at 30 fps, whatever the shape. Whether quality may go below it is for #99.
- **Labels.** A step is labelled by its encoded size: `1080p30` for 16:9, else `2968×1242@60`. Telemetry and the admin view show these labels, and the admin timeline orders them by `maxBitrate`, which is the order of one share's steps.
- **Order by room kind.** What a step down gives up first follows the content hint. Text-like rooms (`detail`, `text`; `maintain-resolution`) keep full size and drop 60 to 30 fps first, then shrink at 30 fps. Motion rooms (`motion`; `maintain-framerate`) keep 60 fps and shrink first, and lose it only at the smallest size. A new or resumed pair starts at the **highest step that fits its fair-share ceiling and the room the budget leaves** (and its capture): one viewer gets the top step, three viewers on 23 Mbps get up to 7.67 Mbps each, nine get up to 2.56. It is not held at a 1080p30 cost: that would open a gaming streamer on an ultrawide at a smaller picture than before. The controller's normal step-down handles a link that can't carry it. A screen whose frame rate crosses the 60 fps cutoff gets its steps built again with different cutoffs up and down (59 to turn 60 fps on, under 50 to turn it off), so a rate that dithers changes nothing, and the sender carries over to the step of the same size at the nearest frame rate rather than the nearest cost, so flips don't walk it down.
- **Fair share.** Every viewer of the streamer is entitled to an equal share of the budget, after what the cameras use. A viewer whose own link holds it lower (it stepped down under pressure and no higher step has held since) can't use all of its share, and takes only what it targets: the rest is divided equally among the others (and a link the larger share then falls under binds in turn). So the others' probes may climb into it without the budget growing. The viewers counted are every connection that hasn't failed or hidden the screen, whether or not its screen sender has started, so a viewer still connecting has its share kept. A probe within the pair's own ceiling is **entitled**: it goes ahead whatever the raw room, and if pairs sitting above their own ceilings have used it up they step down for it (the screen furthest above its ceiling first, then the heaviest), so a late joiner reaches its share without the budget growing. A probe past its ceiling would take what other pairs are entitled to, so it must fit the room once every other viewer is counted at no less than its ceiling (`probeClaim`), or else the budget grows by that probe, on its turn after a clean spell as before (so a clean uplink still grows the budget, and the shares with it).
- **Cameras first.** When the budget is short, cameras step down (to 180p) before any screen does; then the screen furthest above its ceiling, then the heaviest screen (`shedFirst`). A screen's share is worked out after the cameras, at what they target now.
- **Telemetry.** Rung labels, `maxBitrate` and rung-change rows carry the generated steps. A share that changes shape (a window resized) gets its steps built again, carries over as described above, and records the change as `capture` (only if the step it is on changes). The rung label accepts the size form at any size (`96×54@30` too), and the server reads a report's rows one by one, so a row with a label this version doesn't know is dropped, not the whole report. The admin's "starved" flag no longer reads an absolute bitrate: it looks at the steps at or above the one the share started on.
- **Provisional.** The bits per pixel, the 80% rule, the size tiers and the 59/50 fps cutoffs are tuned in the build and may be revisited with real sessions (#52).

## Addendum: the quality modal (2026-10-12)
Blur comes and goes per viewer, so a person can now see why a stream looks the way it does (#104; the design is #93's variant C, with the reason on top). Every streaming tile has a **"quality" button** (shown on hover, focus or tap like the tile's other controls, for your own share too). It opens the repo's dialog (`Sheet`: labelled, focus trapped, closed with Escape) for that share. The modal is **read-only, has no controls** (those wait for the friends test session), and reads the pair about every second.
- **A viewer sees**, top to bottom: the **reason** as a chip with a one-line explanation; last-minute graphs of **bitrate** (with the step's `maxBitrate` as a dashed line) and **resolution**; chips for the **path** (`direct`, or `relay · UDP/TCP/TLS`), loss, round trip, codec, fps and freezes; and a **timeline of step changes**, each with **what made it** (below). Everything but the reason, the step and its cap is what this page itself receives (`getStats` on the receiver).
- **A streamer sees**, on their own share: a graph of **total upload against the video budget**; **one row per viewer** (a taller sparkline of what is sent, the step, and the reason worded from the streamer's side); and a timeline of step changes across all viewers. The budget is the **video** budget (what the senders' target bitrates may add up to, ~2 Mbps of audio kept back), and it is labelled **"(estimate)"** until `UplinkBudget.measured`: it is the 23 Mbps start (ADR 2's 25 Mbps floor less audio) until the uplink has been seen under strain, which is when it drops to what was being sent. Growth from probes doesn't count as a measurement.
- **Reasons.** One pure function, `reasonOf` (src/lib/quality-reason.ts), maps the pair's controller state to one of five reasons, the first that applies:
  1. **Best quality**: the sender is on the highest step its capture feeds.
  2. **Getting started / trying higher**: a probe up is out (it stepped up and hasn't yet held).
  3. **The connection between you** (the streamer's wording: "…between you and *name*"): the newest sample shows strain (loss of 5% or more, a round trip of 500 ms or more, or a bandwidth limit the estimate backs), or the pair stepped down under strain and no higher step has held since (`limited`). The wording says it **may be either end, or the route between them**, because loss and round trip can't tell which.
  4. **Streamer's computer** (the streamer's: "Your computer"): the same, but the encoder reports a CPU limit and no strain. Strain wins when both show, since a CPU limit shows on every pair at once and strain is the pair's own.
  5. **Streamer's upload is shared** (the streamer's: "Your upload is shared"): the next step costs more than the pair's ceiling, so the budget or the viewer's fair share of it holds it (`ceilingOf`). A pair held down by its own link is answered by 3, since a link-limited pair's ceiling is what it targets now.

  Anything else, nothing holding the pair back but the wait for the next probe, is also **Getting started / trying higher**. What the pressure was down to is recorded when a sender steps down (`pressureCause` of the samples that moved it), so the reason stays true while the pair is held there on clean samples.
- **Firefox senders can't say "Streamer's computer", or a bandwidth limit.** They report no `qualityLimitationReason` (ADR 2, telemetry addendum), so the CPU limit and the bandwidth limit the encoder reports aren't seen. A Firefox streamer's pairs show strain from loss and round trip only (as "The connection between you"), and a CPU-bound Firefox encoder reads as the connection or as the wait for the next probe.
- **What made a step change** is told apart from why the pair sits where it does now. The chip is the current reason. Each timeline entry carries the **cause** of that change (`causeOf`): *Screen size changed* (the capture was resized and the steps rebuilt), *Resumed* (the screen came back after being hidden), *Trying higher* (a probe), *Upload is shared* (the budget stepped it down; "Your upload is shared" from the streamer's side), or, for a step down under pressure, what the pressure was down to: *The connection between you* or *Streamer's computer* (the cause recorded from the samples that moved it). So a capture resize on a pair that then sits at the top reads "Screen size changed", not "Best quality". The first step of a stream is not a change and has no cause. A sender that doesn't say (an older page) leaves the entry bare.
- **The step message.** Per pair, each streamer tells each viewer the step its screen sender is on (the label `screenSteps` gives it), that step's `maxBitrate`, the reason and, if it ever moved, the cause of its latest change (optional, one of six words), as one more kind of `signal` payload, `quality` (src/lib/realtime.ts, `stepNoteFields`). `NoteSender` decides when: when any of the four changes, whenever the pair (re)connects, when the page's socket is back after dropping one, and every 30 s for an unchanged state; a send the socket dropped stays due. A message carries a `seq`, numbered per connection (it keeps counting across a share ending and starting again); a viewer **ignores one that isn't newer** than the one it holds (`applyNote`). A viewer that hasn't heard yet shows only what it receives and labels the streamer's side "Streamer's side unknown". Its words are display only: a peer can say anything about its own side, and the server checks only its shape (a label of at most 32 characters, a bitrate under a gigabit, one of the five reasons, one of the six causes).
- **Transport: the room socket's `signal` relay, not an `RTCDataChannel`.** There are no data channels today. The first would add an SCTP m-section to every pair's SDP and renegotiate every established connection, with its own ICE-restart and Firefox behaviour to test, for about 150 bytes every few seconds at most. The relay already carries the pair's other small state (`visibility`) with the rules wanted here: zod-validated (so the allowlist of payload kinds, `signalPayload`, gains `quality`), only between two members of the same room, dropped for a peer in its reconnect grace (so the message is sent again on connect, and every 30 s), rate-limited. It stays far inside ADR 4's limits: 150 bytes against the 64 KB cap, and a message per viewer when something changes (a burst of nine when the budget moves every pair) against `SIGNAL_RATE_LIMIT`'s 400 in 10 seconds. The cost is that the server can read it; it says nothing the server doesn't know of the room, and nothing is stored there.
- **History.** The Mesh keeps the last minute of each screen stream in memory (`QualityHistory`, src/lib/quality-history.ts), fed by the 4-second quality pass (the sender's `getStats` and the receiver's, which was already read for telemetry) so the graphs aren't empty when the modal opens; while the modal is open it reads that pair again about every second (`refreshQuality`: one `getStats` on the receiver, or, on your own share, on each viewer's sender), so the graphs have a point a second. A point is what changed since the reading before: bitrate from the bytes, loss from the packets. The last 20 step changes per stream are kept however old. **Nothing is stored server-side**, and the telemetry rows (30-second windows, above) are unchanged. The buffer is cleared for a stream when the peer leaves or the connection starts over, when the server says their share ended (a viewer's), and when your own share ends or is replaced; freezes are counted from when this page began watching the share, not the receiver's lifetime.
- **Graphs** are the admin's `TimelineChart` (one axis each, 2 px lines, hairline grid, 10 px axis text in a viewBox of the chart's own pixel width so it stays 10 px on screen, ticks that never read the same), with a time axis that counts back from now ("-1m" ... "now") and the series colours of the admin charts. The per-viewer sparklines have no axes; they share one scale so the rows compare.

## Addendum: a seeded estimate and a faster first minute (2026-10-12)
A new connection's bandwidth estimate starts at about 300 kbps and ramps up, and the controller ignores about 8 s of readings and probes up only after about 20 s, so a share looked soft for its first seconds even though a pair already starts on the highest step its fair share allows (#105 decided this; #106 built it). Three changes; the first is narrower than first decided, for the reason measured below.
- **The seed.** A screen pair's estimate is seeded at **90% of the `maxBitrate` of the step it starts on**, and never above the pair's fair-share ceiling (`seedBitrate`, src/lib/quality.ts): 5,400 kbps for 1080p60, 2,700 for 1080p30, 9,603 for 2968×1242@60. It is Chromium's `x-google-start-bitrate=<kbps>`, put by `withStartBitrate` (src/lib/mesh.ts) on the fmtp line of every media codec (VP8, VP9, AV1, H.264, H.265) of the screen's m-section; a codec without an fmtp line gets one (Chromium writes none for VP8, which may be the codec in use), and retransmission and redundancy formats (rtx, red, ulpfec) are left alone. Parameters already there are kept and the key is never doubled.
  - **Which description.** Like the share-audio Opus munging above, it goes into the **remote description the sending side applies**: a sender's encoder and estimate are configured from what the other side's description asks for, so the hint is on the description the *viewer* wrote (its answer to the streamer's offer, or its offer), in the m-section the streamer sends the screen on. The viewer's own pages don't munge anything for it. The camera's section is left alone.
  - **It applies per connection.** The value feeds the estimate of the whole connection, not of one stream, and a connection in this mesh is one viewer pair, so each pair is seeded from its own start step and ceiling. LiveKit does the same for its first seconds ([client-sdk-android #973](https://github.com/livekit/client-sdk-android/pull/973)).
  - **Firefox ignores it**, which is harmless: its pairs start as they did.
  - **Only in the first remote description a connection applies, and never changed.** Measured in Chromium 153 (see below), the hint sets the estimate when the connection is being set up, and *resets* it on a connection that is running: a different value in a later description cut a 6.8 Mbps estimate to the new value at once, and the same value on a live connection (a share added to a pair that is up) often sent the estimate down to a few hundred kbps, from where it took tens of seconds to climb, where with no hint it reaches the share's bitrate in under a second. So a pair is seeded when its screen is in the negotiation that sets it up: for a viewer who joins a share that is on, with the streamer as the side that offers first (the greater user id). The polite side answers the viewer's offer first and sends in an offer of its own, which comes as ICE connects; seeded there, it collapsed as often as not. A share started on a pair that is up, and the polite streamer's pairs, run unseeded, as before. **This is narrower than #105 decided** ("re-seed it when the start step changes"): re-seeding is what resets a running estimate. Letting the polite side add its transceivers before it answers the first offer would put its screen in the first negotiation too; that changes the negotiation and is not done here.
  - **Measured, not yet confirmed in the field.** Old reports said the hint stopped working in some Chrome versions. In Chromium 153.0.8010.12 it works when the description is the connection's first: the estimate reads the seed within 20 ms of connecting and, with moving content, held near the share's bitrate (4 of 4 runs with the streamer offering first), where unseeded it reads 300 kbps for the first 60–270 ms and reaches about 5.5 Mbps by 0.6 s on localhost. That localhost ramp is quick, so the gain on a real link is likely larger, but CI can't show it: the e2e asserts that the browser takes the munged description and which side's carries the hint, not the estimate (src/lib/mesh.ts, e2e/share.spec.ts). The estimate's first seconds in the real session are what the telemetry's `availableBitrate` and the time to settle (below) will show.
- **The first minute.** For the first **60 s** of a screen sender, a share or a new viewer on one (`FIRST_MINUTE_MS`), the controller ignores **1** warm-up reading instead of 2 (`FIRST_MINUTE_WARMUP_READINGS`) and a probe up needs **2** clean samples instead of 5 (`FIRST_MINUTE_UP_SAMPLES`); stepping down is unchanged. `paceOf` (src/lib/quality.ts) picks the pace from how long ago the sender started, and the Mesh keeps that time (`Adaptation.startedAt`): it survives steps built again, a probe and a step down, and a screen hidden and shown again (the same viewer on the same connection), and starts again for a new share (a new capture) and for a new viewer (a new connection). After the minute the normal windows (2 readings, 5 clean samples) and the backoff apply; a failed probe still doubles the wait in the first minute, from 2 samples. Cameras are not sped up. The uplink budget's own clean spell (5 passes before it grows) is unchanged. Provisional like the rest.
- **Time to settle.** A pair *settles* at its **first stable step**: the first window of the sender's reports that ended with no step change in it (a step change ends the window it is in, so a window is a spell on one step), not counting one that a codec change cut short (the next report names another codec). It settled where that spell began, at the time of the last step change before it, or at its start if there was none. Each row a sender reports says how long after the sender started its window ended (`sinceStartMs`, a nullable column since migration 0009, null for receivers and for rows from before), and the admin's `aggregatePairs` (`settledOf`, src/lib/quality-admin.ts) reads it: the pair table of a room in /admin has a **settled in** column ("0s" for a pair that stayed on its start step; a dash until a window without a change has closed, or for rows that don't say). It is the pair's first settling in the room; a later change does not move it, and a second share by the same pair is not counted separately.
- **Watch rooms keep the `motion` hint.** Revisit after the test session if subtitles look bad (#105).

## Addendum: codecs by what the streamer's machine can afford (2026-10-22)
Decided on the map [Pick video codecs by what the streamer's machine can afford](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/125); built in the tickets linked from that map; so far the telemetry fields below (#135), the caps by codec (#136), the start (#137), the codec switch (#138) and how they are shown (#139).

**Why.** In the 2026-10-21 friends session an Edge 155 streamer sent AV1 to two viewers. The encoder reported a CPU limit within 30 s of the second viewer joining, and the share sat at 720p and 6 to 9 fps for about a minute, while round trips were 2 to 9 ms with no loss. The mesh encodes once per viewer (ADR 1), and desktop Chromium leaves hardware AV1 off for WebRTC by default (Windows, Linux; macOS has no AV1 encoder), so AV1 is almost always libaom in software. libwebrtc already runs AV1 screens at its fastest speed, so only pixels and frames were left to cut. Research: [hardware-encode detection](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/126), [fallback codec costs](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/127), [mid-share codec switch](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/128).

**Amends** the codec order of the adaptive quality addendum (AV1, VP9, H.264, VP8 for every sender). That order stays the normal order. Three things now change it, per streamer and per sender kind (screen, camera). Each pair still drops what its viewer can't decode.
- **The start** ([decided](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/131), [Firefox](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/132)):
  - **Firefox streamers** start all their video on **H.264** (OpenH264), or **VP8** on a pair where H.264 isn't available on both sides. They never use AV1 or VP9. Firefox encodes AV1 in software only, and reports no `qualityLimitationReason`, so the switch below couldn't see it. Detected by user agent. Only what Firefox *sends* changes.
  - **Chromium streamers** read `navigator.mediaCapabilities.encodingInfo({type: "webrtc"})` at share start (the capture's size and frame rate) and at camera on.
    - AV1 `powerEfficient` means the normal order.
    - Otherwise, VP9 `powerEfficient` means **VP9 first**.
    - Otherwise, the normal order.

    H.264 hardware alone never moves the start, since that would put nearly everyone on H.264.
  - **Everyone else**, or on an error (Safari, best-effort): the normal order. Firefox's `powerEfficient` is wrong (true for any codec up to 640×480), and Safari's is a fixed per-codec table, so neither is trusted.
  - **No load prediction** (viewers, pixels, fps, cores) until telemetry shows it is needed.
  - **Built** (#137): `chooseStartOrder` (src/lib/codecs.ts) takes the browser (`browserOf`, by user agent) and the capture's width, height, frame rate and the bitrate of the step it starts on (`captureOf`, src/lib/mesh.ts), and answers a `StartOrder`: the order, whether codecs outside it are left out (Firefox's), and the reason (`browser`, `hardware` or `preferred`). Chromium's two `encodingInfo` questions (`video/AV1`, `video/VP9`) share a 500 ms timeout, and an error or a timeout is the normal order.
  - **Where it is chosen.** The room page (`useRoomMesh`, src/lib/room-media.ts) chooses the order *before* the tracks reach the Mesh: when a share or the camera turns on, `StartOrders` (src/lib/start-orders.ts) asks once, keeps the answer while that kind is on, and only then gives the Mesh the order (`setStartOrder`) and the tracks, a share's audio with its screen, so no sender is made before the order is known, the share's screen and audio go out in one negotiation, and the start-bitrate seed is written as before. A Mesh made again during a share is given the kept order with the tracks, with no wait. The Mesh keeps the order per kind until that kind turns off, and `#prefer` hands it to `orderCodecs` for every new transceiver of the kind, late joiners included (and again for those the kind already has), so a viewer that can't decode VP9 still gets the next codec. The codec switch (#138) returns a camera to that kept start order.
  - **The reason a row gives** is the order's reason only while the codec actually sent is the one it is about (`codecReasonOf`): `hardware` for VP9, `browser` for H.264 or VP8. A viewer without VP9 leaves a hardware-VP9 streamer on AV1, and that pair's rows say `preferred`.
  - **Firefox on VP8 is size-capped.** A Firefox streamer on VP8 (Playwright's Firefox has no H.264, so that is what the e2e saw) is held to about 3.1 MP per frame by the `max-fs` of 12288 macroblocks in the negotiation: 2740×1147 of a 3440×1440 capture, below the top share step (2968×1242, 3.7 MP). It keeps the shape and its bitrate cap. H.264's cap in Firefox is not verified.
  - For a Firefox streamer `orderCodecs` leaves AV1 and VP9 out; if the browser has none of the codecs it keeps, it offers what it has.
- **The codec switch** ([decided](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/130); **Codec switch** in CONTEXT.md):
  - **Trigger:** `qualityLimitationReason: "cpu"` on at least half of the streamer's video pairs, in 2 passes in a row (about 8 s), after the warm-up readings. With one viewer, that pair is enough. It fires from AV1 or VP9, at most once per share.
  - **Target:** every one of the streamer's video senders, screen and camera, goes straight to **H.264**, or **VP8** where H.264 isn't on both sides. Never VP9: software VP9 isn't reliably cheaper than software AV1 on screen text. There is no second switch. A CPU-limited H.264 is left to the steps. A pair that negotiated neither H.264 nor VP8 stays where it is.
  - **Mechanism:** `sender.setParameters()` with `encodings[0].codec` (Chromium 119+, Firefox 142+), with no renegotiation. One keyframe, no freeze. With it, `setCodecPreferences` ranks the abandoned codecs last on every video transceiver, or Chromium reverts the pick when a later description's fmtp differs from the seeded one. Pairs are spaced a few hundred ms apart. A browser without `encodings[].codec` (Safari) gets `setCodecPreferences` and a renegotiation. The start bitrate is never written again (the estimate belongs to the connection and survives).
    - **Built** (#138): `watchCodec` (src/lib/quality.ts, next to `step`) is the trigger, a pure function of one pass's readings: it counts one entry per pair (the streamer's link to a viewer) with a video sender past its warm-up, limited if any of the pair's senders (screen or camera) reports a CPU limit, whatever its codec, and it needs a pair on AV1 or VP9 among them. A pass short of it, or with no pair to judge, breaks the run. The Mesh (`#switchCodecs`, `#moveSenders`, src/lib/mesh.ts) preferences every video transceiver with `SWITCHED_START` (H.264, VP8, then AV1 and VP9 last, not left out) and moves each sender, one pair after another `CODEC_SWITCH_STAGGER_MS` (300 ms) apart, through its `link.tuning` chain: `#applyRung` sends `encodings[0].codec` (the pair's first negotiated H.264, else VP8: `switchTarget`) with the cap of the new codec in the same `setParameters`. Its unchanged-check compares the wanted codec (`encodings[0].codec`, which Chromium and Firefox report back), so a pick a later negotiation reverted is made again on the next pass. A browser that does not report the codec back after the first call that carries one is taken not to take it (a later empty read-back is a reverted pick, healed on the next pass): from then on the switch renegotiates (a new offer per pair, which carries the preferences). The seed is only written into a connection's first remote description, so none is written again.
    - **A reading that still names the old codec** (the stats can lag the change by a pass) is left out, up to two readings: `Adaptation.leaving` in the Mesh skips it for the controller and the telemetry, so the window after the `codec` row (which starts from the last reading on the old codec) holds readings of the new codec only, and no row names AV1 after the `codec` row said H.264. `codecReasonOf` gives `cpu` only for a codec the switch moves to; a reading on AV1 or VP9 reads `preferred`.
  - **The steps:** while a switch is still possible, a CPU limit is not pressure for stepping down; strain still is (a sender on AV1 or VP9 before the switch: `cpuHeld`, `RungOptions.ignoreCpu`). A CPU-limited sample is still not a clean one, so it never counts towards a probe up. Once switched, each pair keeps its step, its sample window and warm-up readings reset (as on resume, but what the pair learned of its link, `backoff` and `limited`, stays), and a CPU limit is pressure again.
  - **Scope and reset:** late joiners start on the switched order (`#prefer`). Ending the screen share resets it, and the camera goes back to its kind's start order (#137) by one more `setParameters`, without a switch row (the telemetry window starts over). The screen's sender carries its pick over to its next share, and `#applyRung` puts it back on the start order when that share's first tune finds a codec the Mesh set and nothing pins. A switch fired with only a camera live lasts until the camera turns off.
  - **Test hook:** `cpuLimitedEncoder` and `setCpuLimit` (e2e/cpu-limit.ts) make a test page's video senders on AV1 or VP9 report a CPU limit. It is an init script of the test's own browser context: the app has no such switch.
- **Caps by codec.** A pair actually sending **H.264 or VP8**, whatever put it there, gets **1.5×** each step's `maxBitrate`, since H.264 needs several times AV1's bitrate for the same text. AV1 and VP9 keep today's caps; labels never change.
  - **Which codec:** the one the sender is actually using, not the preference order: its stats (`outbound-rtp` → codec) once it has been read, and before the first reading the negotiated parameters (`getParameters().codecs`, the first media codec). The SDP seed, written before a sender has any, takes the first media codec the description being applied lists for the screen. So a pair that negotiates H.264 starts, and is seeded, on the scaled caps, not 1.5× too low until its first reading. The parameters list no codecs until the description is applied (checked in Chromium and Firefox), which is after a pair's first tune, so a pair that has already started on the unscaled cap and has no reading yet is started again on the scaled one. A sender with no codec known keeps the unscaled cap.
  - **Where it applies:** `codecCap` / `scaleSteps` in `src/lib/quality.ts` are the one place; the Mesh reads every step through them. The scaled cap is what `setParameters` gets, what the upload budget's targets, fair shares and probe checks add up, what a step down fits to the estimate, the seed (90% of it, still never above the ceiling), the step message's `maxBitrate` and the telemetry's `maxBitrate`.
  - **A codec change** re-applies the cap on the next pass through the usual `#applyRung` (its unchanged-check compares the scaled cap). A pair whose scaled cap pushes the targets past the budget is stepped down by the usual budget fit.

**Telemetry and display** ([decided](https://github.com/theHimanshuShekhar/BhayanakCast/issues/133)):
- **Send rows**
  - Every send row carries a codec reason: `preferred`, `hardware`, `browser` or `cpu`.
  - It also carries `encoderImplementation` and `powerEfficientEncoder` (Chromium, exposed while capturing; null elsewhere), and encode time per frame (Δ`totalEncodeTime` / Δ`framesEncoded`, Chromium and Firefox).
  - A change in the encoder fields ends the window, as a codec change does.
  - **Built** (#135, migration 0013): the columns `codec_reason` (enum, null for receivers and older rows), `encoder_implementation`, `power_efficient_encoder` and `encode_ms` are on `quality_samples`, (the telemetry addendum's field list above is unchanged; this addendum adds to it). The Mesh keeps the reason per video sender (`Adaptation.codecReason`): the reason of its kind's start order (#137), given in the rows as `codecReasonOf` says, and `cpu` once the switch lands. A browser that doesn't say leaves the encoder fields null, and a reading that lacks them (Chromium names the encoder only while capturing; a canvas track never does) is not a change. Encode time is null when no frames were encoded. The encoder fields and the reason are for send rows only; the server drops a received row that has them, or a row with a reason it doesn't know, and keeps the rest of the report.
- **The switch row:** a new trigger, `codec`. It ends the window and names the old and new codec with the reason `cpu`. The step is unchanged.
  - **Built** (#135; emitted by the codec switch, #138): `QualityCollector.codecSwitched(id, to, reason)`. The row's `codec` is the codec moved **to**, and the new nullable column `from_codec` the one it **left**; `codecReason` is the reason for the new codec. The window's stats are of the codec it left, so its `qp` is null (QP is only comparable within one codec). The admin's settling skips a `codec` row, as it skips a window a codec change cut short.
- **Quality modal**
  - **Timeline:** "Switched to H.264" (or VP8), caused by "Streamer's computer" ("Your computer" for the streamer).
  - **Codec chip tooltip:** why this codec: "Lighter codec for the streamer's computer" ("for your computer" for the streamer), "Hardware encoder" or "Firefox streams H.264". Nothing extra for `preferred`. The Firefox copy names the codec actually sent, so Playwright's Firefox (no H.264, so VP8) reads "Firefox streams VP8".
  - **Transport:** the step message carries it to viewers.
- **/admin**
  - **Room timeline:** a `codec_switch` event, also in incidents.
  - **Pair table:** the codec cell reads "AV1 → H.264" with the reason. A new encoder column shows hardware or software and the name, or a dash.
  - **Quality charts:** they mark the switch.
- **Built** (#139):
  - **Step message** (`StepNote`, src/lib/quality-reason.ts): two optional fields, `codecReason` (the reason `codecReasonOf` gives for the codec the screen sender is on now) and `codecSwitch` (`{from, to}`, MIME types, kept for the rest of the share once the switch happened, as `cause` is for a step change). A page from before them sends neither and the server's validation still takes its message. A viewer notes a switch only when a message brings one that the message held before did not (`switchHeard`), so one who first hears of the share after the switch sees no entry, as for a step's first message. The streamer notes its own switch for each viewer as it moves that pair's sender.
  - **Quality modal** (`QualityHistory.switched`, `ViewerQuality.switches`, `StreamerQuality.switches`): the timeline merges codec switches with the step changes by time, and the graphs get a hairline at each. The streamer's own modal also shows each viewer's codec in its row, with the same tooltip.
  - **Room timeline:** `codec_switch` is a connection event kind (`codecSwitchEvent`, src/lib/connection-events.ts), reported by the streamer's browser once per share (for the first sender it moves), with the actor as the streamer and no target. It is a kind of the text column, so it needed no migration. It is in the media category, a mark on the streamer's lane, and a row ("Ana's video switched from AV1 to H.264", with why) in the events table and so in incidents.
  - **/admin quality:** `QualityPair` gets `switchedFrom`, `codecReason` and `encoder` (the sender's latest rows that say), and `PairTimeline` gets `codecSwitches`, which the charts mark with the rung changes. The codec cell is "AV1 → H.264" with the reason's words under it (nothing for `preferred`); a pair back on the codec it left (a later share) is no longer shown as switched.
