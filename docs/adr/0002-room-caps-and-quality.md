# 2. Room caps and adaptive stream quality

Date: 2026-09-27 · Status: accepted · Partly superseded 2026-10-11 (the fixed screen ladder, `captureCap`, the download estimate and the heaviest-first step-down; see the last addendum)

## Context
Under the P2P mesh (ADR 1), each streamer uploads one copy of their screen share per viewer, so the streamer's uplink is the limiting factor.

## Decision
- Rooms cap at **10 people**, with at most **3 simultaneous streamers** (screen shares).
- Screen-share quality ladder: **low 720p30 → default 1080p30 → max 1080p60**, with intermediate steps. *Superseded 2026-10-11: steps are now built for each share from its capture, up to about 3.7 MP (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps").*
- Quality is auto-negotiated and continuously adjusted per peer connection based on live connection stats (bandwidth estimate, packet loss, RTT), moving anywhere between low and max.
- Each peer pair negotiates the best codec both sides support (SDP codec preference ordering, e.g. AV1 > VP9 > H.264 > VP8, subject to hardware encode support).

## Consequences
- **Bandwidth assumption:** users have at least 25 Mbps symmetric, and on average 100 Mbps or more.
- Worst-case upload is a streamer sending to 9 peers: 1080p30 is roughly 2.5–4 Mbps per copy (~25–35 Mbps). Their camera and mic add ~5 Mbps. Average (100 Mbps) users comfortably sustain 1080p30, and often 1080p60. Users at the 25 Mbps floor adapt down towards 720p30 when streaming to a full room.
- Worst-case download is 3 streams at 1080p60 (~20 Mbps) plus 9 cameras (~5 Mbps), which fits within the floor. *Superseded 2026-10-11: a share can now be 10.67 Mbps (2560×1440 or an ultrawide at 60 fps), so 3 shares and 9 cameras come to about 37 Mbps, which does not fit the floor. A viewer's receive side is not limited by anything yet; that is for #99 to decide ("How should the quality controller react to a viewer whose incoming bandwidth is short?").*
- Because every link is a separate RTCPeerConnection, quality and codec can differ per viewer — a viewer on a bad link does not degrade others.
- Design copy mentioning "10–15 people" and 15-person capacities must change to 10.

## Addendum: cameras and mics
- Anyone in a room may turn on their camera. Camera default is 360p15, adaptively dropping to 180p under pressure.
- A camera track is paused towards any peer that is not currently rendering that tile (hidden viewers, collapsed layout).
- Mic audio uses Opus for every peer.

## Addendum: screen-share audio
Screen shares request audio through `getDisplayMedia`. Where the browser provides it (tab audio in Chromium; system audio on Windows), it is sent as a separate Opus track at ~128 kbps stereo with voice-processing (AEC/NS/AGC) disabled. Each viewer has their own volume control per share.

## Addendum: screen-share tuning (2026-09-29)
- A share starts only once the server accepts it (at most 3 streamers); the browser's screen picker opens after that, and a share the user cancels there ends at once.
- Browsers open the picker only within a few seconds of the user's click. The client waits at most 3 seconds for the server, and checks `navigator.userActivation` before opening the picker. If either is too late, the share ends and the user sees "couldn't start sharing — try again". A share the browser refuses otherwise (a cancelled picker) ends silently.
- **A cancelled share still counts.** The room saw it as LIVE from the server's acceptance, so it keeps its stream interval (a few seconds long) and its "started sharing" and "stopped sharing" feed entries. Not recording it would need another protocol step: the client would confirm capture before the hub opens the interval, and everyone's LIVE tile would wait for that. That isn't worth it for a few seconds.
- The share's video track gets a `contentHint` from the room kind: `motion` for gaming, watch-party and art rooms; `text` for code rooms; `detail` for music and chat rooms. Its sender's `degradationPreference` follows the hint: `maintain-framerate` for motion, `maintain-resolution` for detail and text.
- Capture asks for at most 1920×1080 at 60 fps, the ladder's top rung; each peer's sender encodes at the rung its connection allows (see the adaptive quality addendum). *Superseded 2026-10-11: capture is the screen's native size up to about 3.7 MP, at up to 60 fps.*
- Share audio is marked `music` and sent with `maxBitrate` 128 kbps. Browsers only encode stereo Opus at that rate when the receiving side asks for it, so the sender adds `stereo=1; maxaveragebitrate=128000` to the Opus parameters of its share-audio m-section in the remote description it applies. The mic stays at the browser's voice defaults.
- Everyone plays a share only while the server says the person is sharing, so a share a host, mod or admin stopped disappears for every viewer even if media still arrives (ADR 15).

## Addendum: adaptive quality and codecs (2026-09-29)
- **Ladders.** A screen share moves along 720p30 (1.5 Mbps), 900p30 (2.2), 1080p30 (3, the default), 1080p45 (4.5) and 1080p60 (6). A camera moves between 360p15 (the default, 500 kbps) and 180p15 (150 kbps). A rung is applied to one peer's sender with `setParameters` (`maxBitrate`, `maxFramerate`, `scaleResolutionDownBy` from the capture's height), so every viewer has a ladder of their own. A rung the capture can't feed (a 720p window; a 30 fps capture) is above the cap and never used. *Superseded 2026-10-11 for screens: the fixed ladder and the height-based cap (`captureCap`) are gone, and a share's steps are built from its capture (see "Addendum: budget-driven quality steps"). The camera ladder stands.*
- **Controller.** Every 4 s the Mesh reads each video sender's `getStats()` (bandwidth estimate, the bitrate actually sent, loss the receiver reports, round trip, the encoder's quality-limitation reason). `step` and `nextRung` (src/lib/quality.ts) are pure functions of the newest samples and the current rung.
  - *Pressure* is loss of 5% or more, a round trip of 500 ms or more, a CPU limit, or a bandwidth limit while the estimate is below 80% of what is being sent. Sending little (a still screen) is never pressure, and neither is a bandwidth limit that the rung's own `maxBitrate` puts on the encoder: Chromium's estimate only probes up to about the configured cap, so it says nothing about headroom above it.
  - It steps down after 2 pressured samples in a row, as far as the estimate says it must (net of what the peer's other video sender sends).
  - It steps up one rung as a probe after a window of 5 clean samples in a row (about 20 s), without asking the estimate for headroom. If the probe shows pressure, the step down brings it back and the next probe waits twice as long (up to 8 times the window); a probe that stays clean for a window resets the wait.
  - The window empties whenever the rung changes, and the first 2 readings of a new or resumed sender are ignored while the estimate ramps up. A rung is applied per sender one change at a time and checked against the encoding on every pass, so a refused change or a resized capture heals by itself.
  - **The thresholds and windows are provisional**, to be tuned after the real-world session (#52); CI only sees localhost.
- **Codecs.** Each side tells the other which video codecs it can decode, in its `hello` and its descriptions (MIME types only). Each side then orders its own senders' codecs with `setCodecPreferences`: AV1, VP9, H.264, VP8 (others after them, then retransmission formats), leaving out what the peer can't decode. It takes effect in the next offer; before the peer has spoken, everything is offered in the same order and the answer keeps the best codec both have. The result is the same whichever side hears the other first. The mixed Chromium↔Firefox choice is covered by unit tests only: e2e pairs are always the same browser. Browsers that lack a codec (Firefox without AV1) simply don't list it. Audio stays Opus. Signalling sizes stay far below the 64 KB cap (ADR 4 addendum): about 24 KB for Chromium's largest offer with all eight m-sections.
- **Dev overlay.** In development builds only (`import.meta.env.DEV`), the room shows each peer's current rung and codec at the bottom left of the stage. Production builds don't contain it.

## Addendum: one uplink for all the pairs (2026-10-02)
- **The problem.** Every pair adapted on its own connection's stats, so a streamer with many viewers started every pair at 1080p30 (3 Mbps each, 27 Mbps for nine), saturated the uplink, and then every pair probed up again independently. The pairs also share one uplink, which no single pair's stats can show.
- **The budget.** The Mesh owns an `UplinkBudget` (src/lib/quality.ts): the most that the target bitrates (the rung's `maxBitrate`) of all the streamer's video senders, screens and cameras, may add up to. It starts at 23 Mbps: this ADR's 25 Mbps floor less 2 Mbps kept for audio (share audio and mics to nine viewers come to about 1.7 Mbps, and aren't in the budget). It then follows measurements:
  - *New pairs*, and a camera shown again after it was paused, start at the highest rung they would otherwise have that the other senders leave room for (never below the lowest rung, so a crowded room can start over budget). The next pass steps the heaviest senders down, a rung at a time, until the targets fit. *Superseded 2026-10-11: cameras step down first, then the screen furthest above its pair's ceiling (see the last addendum).* A pair whose stats read is still running (the per-peer guard, #74) is left out of that, and counts in the budget with what it was last measured sending.
  - *Strain on the uplink* is strain (loss of 5% or more, a round trip of 500 ms or more, or a bandwidth limit the estimate backs; a CPU limit is not strain, since no uplink cures it and it shows on every pair at once) on at least two pairs and at least half of them, in 2 passes in a row. One viewer's bad link stays that pair's own business: it steps down alone and the budget doesn't move. When the uplink is short, the budget drops to 90% of the bitrate the senders were measured sending then (the sum of each sender's `bytesSent` between readings), but by no more than 30% at a time, since a still screen sends next to nothing whatever the uplink does. The senders then step down to fit it.
  - *Probing up* goes ahead when the next rung fits the room left in the budget. *Superseded 2026-10-11 for screens: a probe within the pair's own ceiling goes ahead whatever the room, and pairs above theirs step down for it (see the last addendum).* Beyond it, a probe is admitted only after a clean spell (5 passes without strain on the uplink), and only for the sender with the lowest rung among those that can probe now (a connected pair, clean for a window, below its top step), so a viewer on a mediocre link, or a pair that isn't connected, never holds up the others. The budget grows by just that probe, so past it one pair at a time tries, and it recovers after strain clears. If strain returns before a growth has held for a window, the next clean spell is twice as long (up to 8 times), as for a pair's own probes.
- **`nextRung` and `step` stay pure.** A probe is only a proposal: the Mesh asks the budget before taking it, and if refused the sender's window carries on as it was.
- **Why the budget is not just the bandwidth estimate.** Each connection has its own estimate (`availableOutgoingBitrate`), and the estimates of pairs that share a link are not additive. They also only probe up to about the configured cap (see above), so they say nothing about headroom. The estimate still guides a pair's own step down; what the whole uplink carries is read from what was actually sent when strain is seen, and from clean running otherwise.
- **Provisional.** As for the rest of the controller, the 23 Mbps start, the 90% share, the 30% bound, the "two pairs and half of them" rule and the spells are guesses to be tuned after the real-world session (#52).
- **Encoder parameters are applied only when they change.** A browser that doesn't report `degradationPreference` in `getParameters()` (Firefox, probably Safari) never matched what a screen sender wanted, so `setParameters` ran for every sender on every pass (every 4 s). The comparison now takes such a browser to have what was last set on that sender.

## Addendum: quality telemetry (2026-10-11)
The thresholds above are guesses, and blur comes and goes (one viewer sees every share soft while the others are fine), so every browser in every room records what its video looks like on the wire. There is no notice in the app (the operator's choice); the data is visible to admins only (`/admin`, per room), stored for 90 days (ADR 11 addendum).
- **What.** Each browser reports one row per video sender (screen or camera, to each peer) and one per video receiver (from each peer), every 30 seconds, as averages and deltas over that window, and one more at every rung change, which ends the window with the rung it was on, the one it moved to, and why (`pressure`, `probe`, `capture`, `budget`, `resume`). A row has the rung and its `maxBitrate`, the encoder's `targetBitrate`, sent or received bitrate, `availableOutgoingBitrate`, average QP (Δ`qpSum`/Δframes, comparable only within one codec), the codec, frame size and fps on both ends, `qualityLimitationReason`, loss and round trip, PLI, FIR and NACK counts and keyframes, the receiver's freezes, the selected candidate pair (local and remote `candidateType`, `relayProtocol`), and the browser and its major version (from the user agent, on the server).
- **Both ends report**, because Firefox senders lack `qualityLimitationReason`, `targetBitrate` and `availableOutgoingBitrate`: what a browser lacks is stored as null, never 0, and the receiver's rows are then the only view of that pair. A sender's `qualityLimitationReason` stays `none` under `maintain-resolution` even when starved, which is why `targetBitrate` against the rung's `maxBitrate` is recorded (the admin table flags such a pair as starved).
- **Cadence.** It rides the existing 4-second quality pass (`Mesh.adjust`), which also runs for a page that sends nothing; senders are read there already, receivers add one `getStats` each per pass. A window closes at the first pass within 2 seconds of 30.
- **Transport.** Rows go to the server as batched server-function calls (`reportQualityFn`, like `reportIceFn`), not over the room socket: the hub runs every message on one serial queue and this is database work (ADR 4 addendum on flooding), and a report that fails costs a few rows instead of a reconnect. A page sends at most one report every 5 seconds, 64 rows each.
- **Trust.** The server takes a report only from a signed-in caller who is in that live room, keeps only rows about peers in it, dates rows by its own clock, takes the room's kind from the room, and refuses a user more than 1,500 rows in 10 minutes.
- **QP across codecs.** A window ends when a stream's codec changes, so a row's QP is of one codec, and the admin table averages QP over the pair's latest codec only.
- **Stalls.** Only shares in progress are reported. A receiver of a screen or camera its peer is still sharing (the server's `media.share` / `media.cam`, the same state that decides whether the room plays it, per the screen-share tuning addendum above; a camera this page has hidden is paused towards it and doesn't count) that has received something and then gets nothing still reports (a bitrate of 0); one that never received anything reports no rows. When the peer stops sharing, the receiver sends what its window holds once, as a last (partial) row, and no more rows follow, so a share that ended isn't taken for a stalled one; a new share by the same peer starts a new stream, counting only the bytes that arrive after it was first read. A reconnect drops a pair's open windows but not what is known of who shares what. A rung-change row that ends a zero-length window has null counts and rates, not 0.
- **Leaving the page loses data.** A page that leaves the room (or closes) drops what it hadn't sent: its last partial windows, and the finished rows still waiting out the 5-second batching delay. The server would refuse them anyway, since the page is no longer in the room.

## Addendum: budget-driven quality steps (2026-10-11)
**Supersedes** the fixed screen ladder (720p30 → 1080p60) and `captureCap`, which capped the ladder by the capture's *height*. The first production telemetry (#97) showed the cost: a streamer on a 3440×1440 ultrawide was captured at 1920×804, `captureCap` took every rung above 720p to be above the capture, and the share sat on 720p30 at 1.5 Mbps for the whole session with about 4 Mbps of estimated bandwidth unused. The camera ladder (but for the camera's capture height, which still caps it), the codec order, the controller's thresholds and its step-down on pressure, the probe and its backoff, the first-readings ignore and the CPU-pressure handling are unchanged. The budget's accounting changes in three ways, below: fair shares, cameras first, and entitled probes.
- **Budget.** The streamer's budget is the measured `UplinkBudget` above (23 Mbps to start). The streamer sets nothing.
- **Ceiling per pair.** A pair's ceiling is the lower of its **fair share** of the budget and **what its own link carries** (`ceilingOf`, `pairCeilings` in src/lib/quality.ts). It is worked out in one place, so that a third term, the viewer's receive share, can be added later (#99). The fixed top of 1080p60 / 6 Mbps goes.
- **Capture.** The screen's native size up to about 3.7 MP (2560×1440, 3,686,400 pixels), at up to 60 fps. The cap is by pixel count, not width: 3440×1440 becomes 2968×1242, not 1920×804. `getDisplayMedia` constraints can't say "at most 3.7 MP"; width and height maxes only bound a box, and the browser scales a larger screen down into it keeping its shape. A box of 2970×1440 fits a 16:9 and a 21:9 screen at about the cap, but a 32:9 screen would get 2970×836 (67% of it) and a portrait 1440p one 810×1440 (32%). So where the browser allows it the first request is a box of up to 3840×3840, which delivers the screen at its own shape (a 5120×1440 screen comes as 3840×1080), and the track is then given `applyConstraints` with the box that fits that shape to the cap (`fitCapture`: 3620×1018 for that screen, 2560×1440 for 4K), or the fixed 2970×1440 box if that is refused (`fitScreenCapture` in src/lib/local-media.ts). A capture already under the cap is left alone. Chromium does this (checked in the e2e on a canvas track, whose `applyConstraints` scales like a screen's; Chromium's fake screen returns whatever box it is given, so it can't show the shape); **Firefox keeps the fixed 2970×1440 box**, because resizing a screen track there is unverified (headless Firefox has no screen to capture, and its canvas stand-in refuses `applyConstraints`), so a 32:9 or portrait screen in Firefox stays under the cap. Either way the steps fit the capture to the cap by pixel count themselves and scale down from what was captured with `scaleResolutionDownBy`, which is the same in both.
- **Steps.** Steps are built for each share from its capture (`screenSteps`), lowest first. Each is a size times a frame rate of 30 or 60 (60 only if the capture delivers it). The top size is the fitted capture. Below it come the capture's shape at the pixel counts of 1080p, 900p and 720p, as far as they are well below the top (under 80% of its pixels), so an ultrawide gets ultrawide-shaped steps and a small window gets fewer (a 1280×720 window has one size) and is never upscaled.
- **Bitrate.** `maxBitrate` is pixels × frames per second × **0.0482 bits per pixel** (3 Mbps ÷ 1920×1080×30, so that 1080p30 is 3 Mbps), rounded to 10 kbps. For 16:9 this is about today's numbers (720p30 1.33 Mbps, 900p30 2.08, 1080p30 3, 1080p60 6); 1440p60 is 10.67 Mbps; 2968×1242 at 60 fps is 10.67.
- **Floor.** The lowest step is about 720p's pixels (0.92 MP) at 30 fps, whatever the shape. Whether quality may go below it is for #99.
- **Labels.** A step is labelled by its encoded size: `1080p30` for 16:9, else `2968×1242@60`. Telemetry and the admin view show these labels, and the admin timeline orders them by `maxBitrate`, which is the order of one share's steps.
- **Order by room kind.** What a step down gives up first follows the content hint. Text-like rooms (`detail`, `text`; `maintain-resolution`) keep full size and drop 60 to 30 fps first, then shrink at 30 fps. Motion rooms (`motion`; `maintain-framerate`) keep 60 fps and shrink first, and lose it only at the smallest size. A new or resumed pair starts at the **highest step that fits its fair-share ceiling and the room the budget leaves** (and its capture): one viewer gets the top step, three viewers on 23 Mbps get up to 7.67 Mbps each, nine get up to 2.56. It is not held at a 1080p30 cost: that would open a gaming streamer on an ultrawide at a smaller picture than before. The controller's normal step-down handles a link that can't carry it. A screen whose frame rate crosses the 60 fps cutoff gets its steps built again with different cutoffs up and down (59 to turn 60 fps on, under 50 to turn it off), so a rate that dithers changes nothing, and the sender carries over to the step of the same size at the nearest frame rate rather than the nearest cost, so flips don't walk it down.
- **Fair share.** Every viewer of the streamer is entitled to an equal share of the budget, after what the cameras use. A viewer whose own link holds it lower (it stepped down under pressure and no higher step has held since) can't use all of its share, and takes only what it targets: the rest is divided equally among the others (and a link the larger share then falls under binds in turn). So the others' probes may climb into it without the budget growing. The viewers counted are every connection that hasn't failed or hidden the screen, whether or not its screen sender has started, so a viewer still connecting has its share kept. A probe within the pair's own ceiling is **entitled**: it goes ahead whatever the raw room, and if pairs sitting above their own ceilings have used it up they step down for it (the screen furthest above its ceiling first, then the heaviest), so a late joiner reaches its share without the budget growing. A probe past its ceiling would take what other pairs are entitled to, so it must fit the room once every other viewer is counted at no less than its ceiling (`probeClaim`), or else the budget grows by that probe, on its turn after a clean spell as before (so a clean uplink still grows the budget, and the shares with it).
- **Cameras first.** When the budget is short, cameras step down (to 180p) before any screen does; then the screen furthest above its ceiling, then the heaviest screen (`shedFirst`). A screen's share is worked out after the cameras, at what they target now.
- **Telemetry.** Rung labels, `maxBitrate` and rung-change rows carry the generated steps. A share that changes shape (a window resized) gets its steps built again, carries over as described above, and records the change as `capture` (only if the step it is on changes). The rung label accepts the size form at any size (`96×54@30` too), and the server reads a report's rows one by one, so a row with a label this version doesn't know is dropped, not the whole report. The admin's "starved" flag no longer reads an absolute bitrate: it looks at the steps at or above the one the share started on.
- **Provisional.** The bits per pixel, the 80% rule, the size tiers and the 59/50 fps cutoffs are tuned in the build and may be revisited with real sessions (#52).
